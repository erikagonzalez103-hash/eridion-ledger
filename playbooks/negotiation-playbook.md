# Eridion Glass — Negotiation Playbook

The knowledge base for the Eridion negotiation agent. Tactics are distilled from
Chris Voss (*Never Split the Difference*), the Harvard Program on Negotiation
(*Getting to Yes* — Fisher & Ury), and Michael C. Stone's contractor-side guidance —
applied to a glass subcontractor negotiating with DFW production builders.

**Prerequisite:** the pricing playbook. No negotiation position is taken without
current unit costs, the derived markup, and the margin floor. Numbers first, then
tactics.

---

## Layer 1 — Structure the deal (Harvard PON)

- **BATNA — best alternative to no deal.** Before any negotiation, write down what
  happens if it fails: replace the volume with other builders? absorb the capacity?
  Your BATNA — not your wish — sets your walk-away number. Strengthening your BATNA
  (bidding other builders, custom work pipeline) is often worth more than any
  tactic at the table.
- **Know their BATNA too.** The builder's alternative is requalifying another glass
  sub: onboarding time, insurance/paperwork, schedule risk on active communities,
  warranty continuity. Switching costs are your quiet leverage — a purchasing
  manager who threatens to rebid still has to explain a schedule slip to their VP.
- **ZOPA — the zone between your floor and their ceiling.** If their target price
  is below your cost + margin floor, there is no zone; the correct move is a
  respectful no, not a creative discount.
- **Interests, not positions.** "We need 5% off the price list" is a position. The
  interest behind it may be a corporate cost-down mandate, a specific community
  that isn't penciling, or optics for their boss. Trade against the interest:
  cycle-time guarantees, first-slot scheduling, consolidated billing, or a lower
  price on one plan in exchange for escalation language on materials.
- **Objective criteria.** Anchor to standards neither side controls: material
  price indexes (glass, aluminum), published labor cost data, the contract's own
  change-order clause. "The index moved 11%, our list moved 3%" is harder to argue
  with than "we need more."
- **Trade, never concede.** Every concession has a price tag and gets something
  back: volume commitment, faster payment terms, reduced retainage, more
  communities. A concession given free teaches the builder to ask again.

## Layer 2 — Run the conversation (Voss)

- **Tactical empathy.** Name the other side's situation before arguing yours.
  Purchasing managers are graded on cost-downs and schedule; acknowledge that
  pressure out loud and they stop defending it.
- **Labels.** "It seems like corporate is pushing hard on cost this quarter."
  "It sounds like the last sub burned you on schedule." Labels defuse and draw
  out information. Follow a label with silence.
- **Mirrors.** Repeat their last few key words as a question ("…below last year's
  pricing?") and let them elaborate. Cheapest information-gathering tool there is.
- **Calibrated questions.** Open-ended "how/what" questions that hand them the
  problem: "How am I supposed to take this on at 2020 pricing?" "What happens to
  the schedule if this doesn't get resolved this week?" Never "why" (accusatory),
  never questions answerable with a flat no.
- **The accusation audit.** Open by naming the worst things they could think:
  "You're going to think we're just another sub crying about inflation…" — then
  make the evidence-based case. Deflates objections before they're raised.
- **"No" is not failure.** Questions that invite "no" feel safe to answer: "Is it
  a ridiculous idea to tie glass pricing to the index like your lumber deals?"
  Chase "that's right" (they restate your case themselves), distrust "you're
  right" (a brush-off).
- **Anchoring and the Ackerman plan.** Decide the target number first, open above
  it, plan the retreat: ~65% → 85% → 95% → 100% of the distance to target, in
  shrinking steps, with a non-price ask attached to the final move. Precise,
  odd numbers ($47.35, not $50) read as calculated, not padded.
- **Late-stage "how" questions beat caving.** When they push past your floor:
  "How can I make that work?" forces them to solve your constraint or reveal
  flexibility in theirs.
- **The 7-38-55 reality.** Tone and delivery carry more than the words. The
  calm, curious late-night-DJ voice — especially when delivering a firm no.

## Layer 3 — Scenario modes (the dropdown)

Each negotiation type changes the agent's entire posture:

### 1. Annual price list renewal
The main event. Preparation: line-item health check from the pricing tool, material
index data since last renewal, your win-rate and volume history with this builder,
their switching cost. Open with the accusation audit + objective criteria. Target:
the ranked repricing list from the pricing audit. Concession currency: schedule
priority, volume tiers, multi-community commitments.

### 2. Bidding a new community / plan
Competitive mode. Price from the cost sheet with the derived markup — never "sharpen
the pencil" below floor to buy in without a named strategic reason and a dollar cap.
Ask calibrated questions about the real award criteria: "What would make a sub a
no-brainer for this community besides price?"

### 3. Change order dispute
Documentation war, not a price debate. Evidence: contract clause, dated photos,
signed field directives, the original scope in the bid. Posture: calm, factual,
labels + calibrated questions ("What should I tell my installer who's already
performed the work?"). Never perform disputed extra work without written direction —
that's a pricing-playbook guardrail leaking into the field.

### 4. Payment terms / retainage
Money has a time cost — a 10% retainage held 6 months is a real, computable margin
hit; the agent should compute it and put it on the table as a number. Trade price
against terms: a small list discount for net-15 or reduced retainage can beat a
"win" on price at net-60.

### 5. Scope creep on an existing contract
Catch it at the first occurrence, kindly: label ("It seems like the supers expect
mirror re-sets included…"), then re-anchor to the contract scope and offer the
add-on price. Silence here reprices the whole contract downward.

### 6. Back-charge dispute
Accusation audit first ("You probably expect us to fight every dollar of this…"),
then evidence: completion photos, sign-offs, warranty terms. Concede fast on the
legitimate items — it buys credibility to kill the illegitimate ones.

### 7. Client-facing bid (homeowner sees the bids and picks the sub)
Not a negotiation — a **positioning** exercise. Price is one column; make the other
columns visible: warranty, install lead time, dedicated crew, photos of comparable
work, what "included" actually includes vs. the low bid's exclusions. Three-option
pricing (Enns) works directly on homeowners: base / recommended / premium. The
one-paragraph differentiator matters more than $200 on the number.

## Layer 4 — Builder profiles

One profile per builder, maintained after every negotiation:

- Decision maker(s) and who they answer to; who actually signs off
- % of Eridion annual revenue (leverage context — hardball with a 40% builder is a
  different decision than with a 5% builder)
- Payment behavior: average days-to-pay, retainage terms, back-charge history
- Price history: last 3 renewals, what moved, what they resisted
- Concession log: what we gave, what we got, what they gave
- Relationship notes: supers we work well with, past escalations, sore spots
- Their current pressures: community openings, sub turnover, corporate mandates

## Layer 5 — Prep brief (generated before every negotiation)

One page, reviewable in the truck:

1. Negotiation type (scenario mode) and who's across the table
2. Goal number · opening anchor · walk-away floor (from the pricing tool)
3. Our BATNA / their BATNA in one line each
4. Three calibrated questions to ask
5. Accusation audit opener
6. Their three likely objections, with responses
7. Concession ladder: what we trade, in what order, and what each trade costs

## Layer 6 — Debrief (logged after every negotiation)

Two minutes: what they opened with, where we landed vs. goal, which tactics landed,
what they revealed (pressures, alternatives, mandates), concessions both ways, and
one thing to do differently. Feeds the builder profile.

## Guardrails (hard rules for any agent using this playbook)

1. Never recommend accepting a number below the pricing playbook's margin floor —
   no matter how persuasive the counterparty's case — without flagging it as a
   floor violation and requiring an explicit strategic override.
2. Every recommended number cites its basis (cost sheet + markup, index data, or
   builder history).
3. Tactics serve legitimate business honestly: no fabricated competing offers, no
   invented costs, no misrepresenting facts. Leverage comes from real numbers and
   real alternatives.
4. When the counterparty is a homeowner (scenario 7), the agent drafts in plain,
   warm language — tactics tuned for purchasing managers read as manipulative to
   consumers.
